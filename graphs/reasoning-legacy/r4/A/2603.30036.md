## Limitations and conditions

- "Our environments are relatively simple, and hence perhaps our empirical results do not generalize to real LLM post-training." (7, line 266) — scope of the whole empirical validation.
- "Although our experiments are limited to toy environments, the framework provides a tool for reasoning about these risks before training begins." (8, line 276) — same, restated in the conclusion.
- "we remained in toy environments largely because of the difficulty of learning against in-conflict rewards in more complex settings." (6.3, line 262) — the toy setting is partly forced by optimisation failure.
- "Our definitions rest on the assumption that models have a strong inductive bias towards transparency due to pre-training on human language" (4.1, line 94) — core assumption of the framework.
- "We assume that $\pi_{\text{sem}}(\text{CoT})\in\mathcal{S}$ —that whatever information the model extracts from its CoT can, in principle, be expressed in natural language. This is a strong assumption" (A.2, line 306) — second core assumption, flagged as strong by the authors.
- "Definitions are all in the tabular RL case. We do not model neural network learning or generalization" (B.8, line 745) — limit of the formal model.
- "if a policy does not achieve high enough $R_{\text{cot}}$ and $R_{\text{out}}$ performance, we will not see issues with transparency." (4.2, line 136) — the in-conflict prediction holds only conditional on successful optimisation.
- "Hence, whether a drop in monitorability will actually be observed is unclear. For example, many modern models are trained with length penalties without any observed drop in monitorability" (7, line 268) — limits the practical claim about length penalties.
- "we expect in many cases it may be unclear which category a pair of rewards falls into" (7, line 270) — classification of real reward pairs is not operationalised.
- "Note that we could not test the framework’s prediction that these rewards actively improve monitorability" (6.2, line 248) — the aligned prediction is untested.
- "For the setting where we see consistent monitorability drops, the behavior is trivial—the policy simply outputs empty reasoning." (6.2, line 250) — one in-conflict case is trivial unmonitorability.
- "models slowly drop in monitorability when training against some orthogonal rewards with certain prompts, even though this does not help reward performance. This fits the dynamic hypothesized by Schoen et al. (2025), which our framework does not capture." (6.2, line 252) — an observed effect the framework misses.
- "It is generally hard to prove a negative (i.e. “X cannot be learned”), but we provide several lines of circumstantial evidence for this claim." (6.3, line 258) — strength of the "hard to optimise" finding.
- "an encouraging sign for the viability of CoT monitoring, though not a guarantee against more capable optimization in the future." (8, line 276) — scope of the difficulty finding.
- "We switched to token-level rewards (CAPO) after standard GRPO failed to optimize in-conflict reward pairs" (6.3, line 262) — the results depend on token-level credit assignment.
- "we run hyperparameter sweeps and select the policy that performed highest on $R_{\text{cot}}+R_{\text{out}}$ across our sweep." (6, line 222) — monitorability is reported for the best-optimised policy per pair.

## Other findings

- Monitorability here is a coarse task-topic classification (is the CoT about a coin, about a backdoor), not detection of a specific misbehaviour. Results do not transfer directly to richer monitoring tasks.
- The draft's `pressure` value `training-incidental` misses that the CoT-monitor reward is a monitor folded into training. That is the strongest of the pressure categories, and it is one of the two in-conflict rewards in each environment.
- The draft says "monitorability does *not* survive this optimisation pressure". The full text conditions this on the policy reaching high reward on both terms. It also notes that the paired in-conflict rewards were often not learned at all.
- Authors are at Google DeepMind (title block, line 4).
