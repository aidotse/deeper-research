- `method name`: confirmed — dependency-graph framework (the paper gives it no other name; the finding is called the "decomposed-to-composed asymmetry") — "we formalize compositional reasoning using a dependency-graph framework" (line 20)
- `method summary`: corrected — Models a task as a DAG of skill invocations, and a reasoning trace as a topological ordering of that DAG. This gives three levels. Level I is a single-skill chain, which tests horizon. Level II is a multi-skill chain, which adds skill switching. Level III is a branch-merge graph, which adds non-local dependencies. The study compares GRPO with binary final-output reward under two training regimes: RL-Atomic, on single skill invocations, and RL-Compound, on multi-step tasks. Both use the same number of steps and train at lengths 5-10, with evaluation up to length 30. The tasks are from DSR-Bench. Section 5 gives a coverage-based explanation. Lemma 5.1 bounds compound failure by a coverage factor C_T(q) times atomic error. A reverse bound holds under a unique-source-node, non-recoverability assumption. — "For each base model, we compare RL-Atomic, trained only on atomic tasks, with RL-Compound, trained only on compound tasks. Both use Group Relative Policy Optimization [4] with binary final rewards, while keeping the number of training steps the same." (line 124); "Models are trained on small problem lengths (5-10) and evaluated on larger ones (up to 30)." (line 112)

## Limitations and conditions

- "We primarily evaluate compound tasks using final-output accuracy." (line 230) — intermediate states are not checked. The authors found that requiring them lowered accuracy.
- "using Structured Outputs with JSON also reduced accuracy, likely due to added formatting burden" (line 230) — the output format was chosen for accuracy, which affects the results.
- "our empirical evaluation focuses on controlled data-structure tasks and broader validation on real-world benchmarks remains an important direction" (line 230) — scope. The tool-calling evidence is a single pilot.
- "Performance improves only modestly, suggesting that composed training alone is insufficient when new reasoning skills are required." (line 168) — composed training does not carry to unseen skills.
- "composed-task training remains robust, though its gains depend on structural difficulty" (line 168) — a condition on robustness under structural shift.
- "We note RL-Compound also degrades sharply as problem length increases, showing that non-local coordination is a stronger bottleneck" (line 164) — RL-Compound does not solve Level III at length.
- "Assuming every context in the support of $\mu_{t}$ has nonzero probability under $q$ for every $t\in[T]$" (line 188) — an assumption of Lemma 5.1.
- "For stochastic policies, we assume the output distribution depends on the rollout history only through the current state" (line 184) — a Markov assumption in the theory.
- "Assume each compound task has a unique source node, so its initial decision context is a singleton. Therefore, under a non-recoverability assumption" (line 208) — the assumptions behind the reverse-transfer argument.
- "Assuming $q$ puts no mass on contexts outside the support of $q_{1}^{\rho_{T}}$" (line 212) — the reverse bound only covers atomic cases that the first step of compound tasks covers.
- "We use instruct models for their instruction-following ability, while excluding models already heavily post-trained for reasoning." (line 124) — scope: no reasoning-tuned models.
- "additionally evaluate two more models from different families and sizes: OLMo-3-7B-Instruct [15] and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct [6] on multi-skill chains" (line 124) — cross-family evidence covers Level II only. Levels I and III, and the pilot, use one model, Qwen3-4B-Instruct.

## Other findings

- Steps are matched, but the volume of supervision is not. Each compound example contains several skill invocations, and an atomic example contains one. So RL-Compound may see more skill practice per step. The paper does not discuss this as a confound in the sections I read. This is my observation, not the authors'.
- The reverse direction ("compound transfers back to atomic") is shown as RL-Compound staying comparable to RL-Atomic on atomic tasks, not as a separate transfer gain. At Level III, all methods are near ceiling on atomic tasks (line 164), so that level gives no test of reverse transfer.
- The SFT vs RL comparison (line 154) uses programmatically templated CoT traces at Level II only. It supports "RL generalises in length better than this SFT", not a general SFT-vs-RL claim.
- No numbers are given in the text for any result; they are in Figures 3-9. A row quoting magnitudes would need the figures.
