- `method summary`: corrected — For each prefix of a sampled chain of thought, the method estimates the model's predictive distribution over discrete answer labels. It does this by Monte Carlo: N = 16 continuations per prefix, at checkpoints every 16 tokens, with a deterministic answer parser. The sequence of these distributions is the trajectory. Propositions 1-2 and Corollary 1 show that endpoint prediction and entropy trajectory are deterministic functions of it, but do not identify it; this is the sense of "strictly finer". Each trace is then summarised by a dynamical profile with four parts: exploration (effective support), revision (dominance switches and returns), motion (total-variation path length, its concentration, and directness) and commitment (when a hypothesis stays above a threshold τ). — "The answer-distribution trajectory is a strictly finer representation of the reasoning process than either endpoint prediction or entropy trajectory." (line 99); "In practice, we use $N=16$ independent continuations per prefix unless otherwise stated." (line 369)

## Limitations and conditions

- "Our analysis is computationally expensive, developing cheaper approximations of answer-distribution trajectories is therefore an important direction for future work." (line 261) — cost limits scale.
- "Moreover, the comparisons in Section 5.3 are only descriptive, and future controlled studies could better isolate the effects of architecture, scale, and post-training." (line 261) — the "training and inference choices reshape profiles" result is correlational, not causal.
- "Lastly, whether the information that answer-distribution trajectories provide can translate into practical gains remains an open question." (line 261) — no downstream benefit is shown.
- "We focus on reasoning tasks with a discrete answer space $\mathcal{A}$ ." (line 271) — scope: the method needs a discrete, parseable answer space.
- "Invalid or unparsable outputs are mapped to a special null answer category." (line 289) — the measured distributions depend on the parser.
- "a maximum generation length of 600 tokens" (line 345) — chains of thought are short. Most models are small, non-reasoning base or instruction-tuned models; only DeepSeek-R1-distilled-7B is RL-trained. The findings may not carry over to long-CoT reasoning models.
- "spaced uniformly at stride $s=16$ , and always including the final prefix length of the trajectory" (line 377) — trajectories are sampled every 16 tokens, so switches finer than that are not seen.
- "Unless otherwise specified, decoding uses:" (line 333) — continuations use the same T = 0.7 sampling. So each "predictive distribution" is the distribution under that decoding configuration, not the model's distribution in general. Section 5.3 shows that temperature itself shifts the profiles.
- "Changes are standardized by the across-condition standard deviation of the corresponding metric." (line 255) — the Section 5.3 effects are reported in standardized units over matched comparisons. In the mapped places, no number of matched pairs per comparison is given.

## Other findings

- The "efficiency" profile in Table 2 comes from short-CoT model-task pairs averaging 18.6% accuracy and 56 tokens, against 78.8% and 240 for the high-accuracy pairs (lines 245, 247). So "different objectives favour different profiles" is confounded with low accuracy and short output. The paper does not separate these.
- In the same table, R_direct is +1.30 for short CoT and -0.14 for high accuracy (lines 245, 247). Short traces are thus more direct, which sits oddly with "more frequent hypothesis switching". The paper's prose at line 237 does not mention directness. This is an observation from the table, not something the authors discuss.
- The "strictly finer" claim is a mathematical corollary about representations: two non-injective maps (line 99). It is not an empirical finding. The empirical part is only that the non-identifiability "arises in practice" (line 193).
