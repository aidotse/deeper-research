# Map: 2512.18311 — Monitoring Monitorability

## Method

- L4 (title): "Monitoring Monitorability" — the paper's title and central concept
- L20 ("Chain-of-Thought Monitoring"): "Monitoring the chain-of-thought (CoT) of today's reasoning models has proven effective for detecting misbehavior" — what CoT monitoring is
- L22 (monitorability vs faithfulness): "we argue, in a similar vein to Baker et al. [5], Emmons et al. [12], that establishing faithfulness is not necessary for most practical purposes. Instead, our interest should lie in an observation's monitorability" — distinction between faithfulness and monitorability
- L32 (metric innovation): "we adapt a metric (g-mean^2 = TPR×TNR) to suit our needs" — the monitorability metric for handling class imbalance
- L36 (evaluation archetypes): "We distinguish between three conceptually different kinds of monitorability evaluations (intervention, process, and outcome-property)" — the three evaluation types
- L60-62 (intervention approach): "we apply a controlled intervention to the agent's environment or input, measure whether the agent's behavior changes due to the intervention, and then ask whether a monitor can infer a specific change in reasoning" — intervention evaluations
- L66-68 (process approach): "We construct problem settings that have a small known set of admissible solution paths. For example, for certain math problems, any correct solution must invoke one of a small number of specific theorems" — process evaluations
- L71-72 (outcome-property approach): "We design environments for which it should be possible to unambiguously label some property of the model's outputs" — outcome-property evaluations
- L132-137 (minimal-criterion TPR): "we generally cannot tell whether the behavior was caused by the intervention or would have occurred anyway due to the baseline rate Pr(Y=1|X=0). What we can estimate from the two arms is the intervention's total effect, TE = E[Y|X=1] - E[Y|X=0]" — handling causal attribution in intervention evals

## Setup

- L6 (authors and affiliation): "Melody Y. Guan, Miles Wang, Micah Carroll, Bowen Baker — OpenAI" — paper authorship
- L293 (model lineup): "We evaluate various OpenAI models (GPT-5 Thinking, OpenAI o3, OpenAI o3-mini, OpenAI o4-mini) across all our evaluation environments, as shown in Figure 1" — models evaluated
- L293-294 (external models): "we evaluated some of the most prominent non-OpenAI reasoning models: Claude 3.7 Sonnet (Thinking), Deepseek R1-0528-Qwen3-8B, and Kimi K2 Thinking" — external reasoning models tested
- L78-80 (evaluation taxonomy): "13 evaluation environments organized along two axes: (i) the evaluation archetype (intervention, process, or outcome-property), and (ii) the behavioral regime they probe" — evaluation taxonomy structure
- L336 (capability metrics): "We measure the mean pass rate over GPQA-Diamond, AIME 2025, and CodeForces (with and without tools) and plot this against the mean monitorability" — capability measurement approach
- L413-429 (hierarchical bootstrap): "For each bootstrap replicate, we resample instances (outer bootstrap) and resample trajectories within instances (inner bootstrap), and recompute metrics end-to-end" — standard error computation methodology

## Headline results

- L12 (aggregate monitorability): "most models are fairly, but not perfectly, monitorable" — overall frontier model monitorability assessment
- L12 (CoT length effect): "longer CoTs are generally more monitorable" — relationship between CoT length and monitorability
- L12 (RL effect): "RL optimization does not materially decrease monitorability even at the current frontier scale" — RL training effect on monitorability
- L16 (CoT vs action monitoring): "CoT monitorability—which we measure with g-mean^2 (product of true positive rate and true negative rate)—tends to increase with the agents' CoT length. Note that the g-mean^2 of a random monitor would be 0.25" — quantitative monitorability measurements
- L16 (action-only monitoring): "The performance of monitors given access to only model actions (tool calls and final answers) is middling; instead, even only giving monitors access to the CoT allows them to significantly outperform action-only monitors" — action-only monitoring performance
- L16 (OpenAI models outperform): "When tested at the maximum reasoning effort available through each API, OpenAI models tend to have higher CoT monitorability relative to external models we tested" — frontier model comparison
- L44 (monitorability tax): "one can often choose to switch to a smaller model at higher reasoning effort to obtain much higher monitorability at only a small capability hit, i.e. a 'monitorability tax'" — monitorability-capability tradeoff
- L349 (monitor scaling): "stronger monitor models achieve higher monitorability scores for the same agent, increasing g-mean^2 by nearly 40% from o3-mini to GPT-5 Thinking at various reasoning efforts" — monitor capability effect on monitorability
- L349 (monitor reasoning effort): "when increasing its test-time compute, it's able to substantially improve monitorability. Going from the lowest to the highest reasoning effort setting, we can improve monitorability by ~12%" — monitor reasoning effort scaling
- L355 (follow-up question effectiveness): "by asking a model follow-up questions and giving the monitor access to the follow-up CoT, we are able to improve monitorability substantially across many of our environments" — follow-up question improvement
- L309 (CoT obfuscation detection): "we find it reassuring that our evaluations were able to catch simple obfuscating models" — evaluation suite catching obfuscation

## Limitations

- L25 (fragility concern): "there are many reasons to expect CoT monitorability lessen over time, e.g. from length penalties that incentivize CoT compression during RL, accidental or indirect CoT pressure from different stages of training, less reliance on CoT for larger models" — potential monitorability degradation mechanisms
- L301 (OpenAI monitor bias): "One limitation of these results is that we only tested monitorability with OpenAI models serving as monitors. This leaves open the possibility that the monitorability results for OpenAI models may be inflated due to shared grammars or idiosyncrasies between model and monitor" — potential bias from using same vendor for monitor and agent
- L329 (small-scale RL experiments): "These results only represent two experiments and more thorough study is needed" — limited RL experiment scale
- L340-341 (capability evaluation limitations): "our results only consider capability evaluations that are somewhat in-distribution for these models; therefore, the tradeoffs may differ when considering out-of-distribution generalization. Finally – with regards to the 'monitorability tax' – we did not consider potential latency costs" — OOD generalization concerns and latency tradeoffs
- L341 (pretraining experiment seed): "A major limitation of these experiments is that we only trained with a single seed at each model scale. The 'medium' sized model seems to be very off trend for monitorability, despite the fact that it behaved normally in our capability evaluations" — single-seed pretraining experiments
- L358-359 (follow-up limitations): "In some datasets, such as Scruples, where the intervention operates through subtle framing rather than explicit content, it is difficult to write a meaningful tailored follow-up question, and models tend to deny being influenced when asked. In this setting, follow-up questions can actively degrade monitorability" — follow-up question limitations
- L283-284 (outcome-property label noise): "The main challenge in creating outcome-property monitorability evaluations is ensuring that the process for assigning ground-truth harmfulness labels is low-noise and unambiguous. We encountered this in both ShadeArena and OAI-repo-sabotage" — label noise in outcome-property evaluations
- L382 (CoT necessity vs verbalization): "Our evaluations do not disentangle the propensity to verbalize versus the necessity to verbalize, and so it could very well be that sycophantic tendencies (if any) are 'baked in' into the model weights such that they do not need to be verbalized" — cannot distinguish learned behavior from verbalization patterns
- L382 (incomplete coverage of degradation): "there are many other processes that we did not investigate" — mechanisms for monitorability degradation not fully explored

## Assumptions and conditions

- L22 (monitorability not about faithfulness): "our notion of monitorability does not require a model's externalized reasoning to faithfully reflect its internal reasoning process" — key assumption about what monitorability measures
- L56 (two-sided nature): "monitorability is a two-sided property: it depends both on the behavior of the model we're trying to evaluate, and the capacity of the monitor to extract useful information from such behavior" — monitorability depends on both agent and monitor
- L74 (behavior requirement for evaluation): "All of our evaluations require that the model first do a specific behavior before we can ask if a monitor can detect that behavior" — models must exhibit the behavior for monitorability to be measurable
- L155-164 (minimum criteria for intervention evals): "A minimally useful monitor should satisfy: (1) Specificity (standard): A good monitor should never trigger when X=0 or Y=0, (2) Sensitivity (non-standard): When X=1 and Y=1, a good monitor should trigger at least as often as the minimum fraction of positives attributable to the intervention" — specific criteria for intervention monitor performance
- L187-188 (intervention eval dropout): "we only compute the metric over instances where TE>0. In order to reduce the contribution of exceedingly small effect datasets, when taking the mean over many evaluations, we drop an intervention dataset if the number of problem instances with TE≤0 exceeds 90% of the dataset" — filtering criteria for undefined intervention cases
- L245-246 (process eval limits): "without full mechanistic explanations for model behaviors, it is not possible to directly determine whether a model's externalized reasoning truthfully reflects its internal reasoning process. But by using carefully constructed settings in our process evals, we aim to get as close as possible" — process evaluations cannot definitively prove internal reasoning
- L251 (process eval negatives): "One downside of this archetype is that there is no way to soundly define negatives: if the model gets an incorrect answer, it may still have correctly applied a solution path but simply made a small mechanical mistake" — no clear negatives in process evaluations
- L252 (process eval metric): "for our g-mean^2 calculation for this archetype, we only consider the sensitivity (TPR), setting specificity (TNR) to 1" — TNR set to 1 in process evaluations
- L284-285 (outcome-property calibration): "It is common in control work to report the monitor's recall on the dataset in question when calibrated to achieve a certain false positive rate on neutral data. We did not calibrate in this way and leave it to future work" — outcome-property evaluations lack FPR calibration

## Dimensions

- **Evidence**: L11 (empirical): "To measure and track monitorability, we propose three evaluation archetypes (intervention, process, and outcome-property) and a new monitorability metric, and introduce a broad evaluation suite. We demonstrate that these evaluations can catch simple model organisms trained to have obfuscated CoTs" — empirical evaluation of monitorability across multiple architectures and training procedures

- **Measures**: L19 (monitorability): "What property the paper's headline evaluation actually measures. For safety work the important distinction is faithfulness (does the stated reasoning reflect the computation that drove the answer) versus monitorability (can a monitor reading the trace detect a target behaviour)" — paper measures monitorability, not faithfulness

- **Elicitation**: L308 (explicitly elicited obfuscation): "We modified a standard internal RL setup to explicitly reward CoT obfuscation in various ways, similar in methodology to [39, 5, 4] though with a more realistic setup using a broad task distribution and beginning with our standard models that are not explicitly finetuned for obfuscation prior to RL" — obfuscated behaviors deliberately trained in; natural behaviors from frontier models observed observationally

- **Pressure**: L305-310 (training pressure during RL): "To probe this, we modified a standard internal RL setup to explicitly reward CoT obfuscation in various ways... We experiment with OpenAI o4-mini sized models, and we show the impact of this obfuscation training on monitorability in Figure 9. In particular, one run (yellow) was rewarded for generic CoT obfuscation" — tests monitor-related pressure applied during RL training to see if evaluations can detect degradation
