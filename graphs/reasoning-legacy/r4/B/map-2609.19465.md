# Map for 2609.19465: Compositional Reasoning in Language Models under Reinforcement Learning Post-Training

## Method

- L13-19 (Problem framing): "Compositional reasoning is a prerequisite for general intelligence. A model cannot merely learn individual skills; it must reuse and combine them systematically to solve unseen, more complex problems." — Motivates study of skill composition under RL
- L20 (Framework): "To address this gap, we formalize compositional reasoning using a dependency-graph framework. We define skills as reusable primitives required to solve reasoning tasks, and use dependency graphs to model the compositional structure among them." — Formalizes compositionality through directed acyclic graphs
- L62-84 (Dependency graph formalization): "For a task instance $\tau \in \mathcal{T}$, let the dependency graph $G_\tau = (V_\tau, E_\tau)$ be a finite directed acyclic graph, where each node represents a skill invocation and each edge indicates that the output of one invocation is required as input to another." — Formal definition of compositional structure
- L86-100 (Three compositionality levels): "Level I: Single-skill chain tests repeated application of one skill; Level II: Multi-skill chain requires heterogeneous skill composition; Level III: Branch-merge graph adds non-local dependencies" — Hierarchical decomposition of compositional complexity
- L114-128 (Main experimental design): "We compare RL post-training on decomposed skills and on composed tasks using five data-structure domains of varying difficulty: Array, Binary Search Tree, Bloom Filter, Hashmap, and Heap" — Controlled comparison between atomic and compound training

## Setup

- L115-121 (Atomic vs compound tasks): "An atomic task corresponds to a decomposed skill: a single skill-invocation applied to a given state. A compound task corresponds to a composed task: a sequence of skill invocations, where intermediate states must be carried forward." — Distinguishes decomposed vs composed supervision
- L123-125 (Models and training): "We use Qwen3-4B-Instruct as the main Base model, and additionally evaluate two more models from different families and sizes: OLMo-3-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on multi-skill chains" — Cross-model evaluation with consistent setup
- L125 (RL method): "Both use Group Relative Policy Optimization with binary final rewards, while keeping the number of training steps the same" — Standardized RL training across conditions
- L132-133 (Horizon definition): "Note this is distinct from problem length, which denotes input size; this setting isolates horizon generalization, where horizon is the number of sequential skill invocations." — Distinguishes composition length from problem complexity
- L142-143 (Multi-skill composition): "We instantiate this setting by adding deletion as an additional skill across the five data-structure domains. Thus, RL-Atomic trains on single-step insertion or deletion, while RL-Compound trains on multi-step tasks with randomly interleaved insertions and deletions." — Adds skill switching to horizon generalization

## Headline results

- L126-128 (Main finding): "Atomic training often improves atomic-task performance, but does not reliably transfer to compound tasks. In contrast, compound training largely preserves or improves atomic-task performance while substantially improving compound-task performance." — Central decomposed-to-composed asymmetry
- L136-137 (Level I): "Both RL-Atomic and RL-Compound consistently improve over the base model, but the decomposed-to-composed asymmetry persists: RL-Atomic gives only marginal gains on compound tasks" — Asymmetry even with single repeated skill
- L146 (Level II): "With heterogeneous skill composition, RL-Compound clearly improves compound-task performance, whereas RL-Atomic provides little or no gain over the base models." — Asymmetry pronounced when skills differ
- L164-165 (Level III): "On compound tasks: RL-Atomic gives little improvement over the base model and quickly collapses to near-zero accuracy, whereas RL-Compound performs substantially better." — Asymmetry extreme with non-local dependencies
- L220-222 (Pilot benchmark): "Figure 9 shows the same asymmetry: decomposed-skill training does not generalize well to compound tasks, whereas composed-task training performs well on both tasks" — Asymmetry appears in Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard

## Limitations

- L167-169 (Structural shift scope): "Performance improves only modestly, suggesting that composed training alone is insufficient when new reasoning skills are required" — Composed training doesn't guarantee transfer to unseen skills
- L154-155 (SFT vs RL): "SFT performs well in-domain but degrades sharply on larger out-of-domain instances, whereas RL shows better length generalization" — Generalization advantage specific to RL training
- L228-231 (Intermediate state measurement): "We primarily evaluate compound tasks using final-output accuracy. Although intermediate-state accuracy could be informative, our preliminary studies found that requiring intermediate outputs reduced final accuracy" — Final-output-only evaluation masks reasoning failures

## Assumptions and conditions

- L68-84 (Formal assumptions): "We assume a set of reusable skill primitives $\mathcal{K}$. For simplicity, we assume that $G_\tau$ has a unique sink node $v_{\text{sink}} \in V_\tau$." — Binary final reward and unique task output
- L178-184 (Policy assumptions): "Assume the output distribution depends on the rollout history only through the current state; probabilities include policy randomness. For stochastic policies, all probabilities and expectations below are also over the policy randomness." — Markovian policy assumption
- L194-200 (Coverage-based bound): "Let $\mu_t$ be the distribution of $Q(s_t)$ conditioned on there being no earlier mistakes. Assuming every context in the support of $\mu_t$ has nonzero probability under $q$ for every $t \in [T]$, define the coverage factor $C_T(q)$" — Coverage conditions for transfer bound
- L206-216 (Reverse-transfer assumption): "Assume each compound task has a unique source node, so its initial decision context is a singleton. Assuming $q$ puts no mass on contexts outside the support of $q_1^{\rho_T}$" — Context distribution alignment for reverse transfer

## Dimensions

**Evidence**: L123-125 uses Group Relative Policy Optimization with binary verifiable rewards; L194-200 provides theoretical analysis with formal bounds on transfer failure — "proven" transfer bounds under stated coverage assumptions plus "empirical" results on five algorithmic domains

**Measures**: L126-128 measures whether trained skills transfer to composed tasks; L194-216 bound error rates through coverage factors — "faithfulness" through matching intermediate state distributions and "monitorability" through step-level accuracy on decision contexts

**Elicitation**: L122-127 trains either on atomic tasks only or compound tasks only; L125 standardizes training steps to isolate effect of composition — "elicited" through curriculum design where decomposed tasks never expose composition patterns

**Pressure**: L125 keeps training steps equal between RL-Atomic and RL-Compound; L178-200 assumes reward signal only at final output — "training-incidental" signal where intermediate states are learned only through successful composition, no explicit intermediate supervision
