## Limitations and conditions

- "we only study this phenomenon on a single model family and two model sizes. Thus, we are unable to make strong claims about how this phenomenon may change across different sizes and training pipelines." (Sec. 5.1, l.140) — scope: Qwen3 4B/8B only.
- "our results should not be taken as representative example of how obfuscation could occur in a production RL environment, but rather a specific example of a failure mode" (Sec. 5.1, l.142) — the environments are constructed, with obviously leaked scores and easy high-reward answers.
- "Emmons et al. (2025) show that obfuscation is less frequent in harder tasks that necessitate CoT" (Sec. 5.1, l.144) — the tasks are not CoT-load-bearing; generalisation when the behaviour requires CoT is untested.
- "For all datasets, summary penalisation is stricter; simply mentions of the additional information is flagged" (l.84) — condition on the output-only result: the summary penalty is strict and also includes a coherence/relevance clause, while the CoT monitor is lenient.
- "in regimes where models produce highly degenerate reasoning traces, the absolute detection rate may be a noisy signal in either direction" (App. I.1, l.469) — monitor false positives on degenerate CoTs; the authors say comparing across conditions "mitigates but does not eliminate this concern".
- "Summary penalisation generalises to in-distribution obfuscation of the CoT in the narrow majority of model-dataset pairs" (l.104) — the in-distribution output-to-CoT effect is not uniform.
- "In each case, we performed the experiment over four seeds. In the main text, we present representative results" (l.100) — main-text figures show one representative seed; full seeds in appendices.
- "We apply two alterations to the datasets: (i) we wrap the MCQ and its choices in <question> XML tags" (l.74) — the training formats are modified; the unformatted-prompt test addresses this for CoT-side obfuscation.

## Other findings

- No headline numbers appear in the mapped body text. The results are reported as training curves (Figs. 4, 5) and examples, so a row should not attach a percentage.
- The obfuscation takes several forms: feigned uncertainty, vacuous text, and CoTs that look like realistic engagement (l.124). The authors note this makes detection hard "when ground truth is unavailable".
- The authors attribute the output-to-CoT spillover to Drori et al.'s two mechanisms (parametric and conditional feedback spillover, l.136). They do not separate the two mechanisms experimentally.
- Mixed signals in the high-stakes setting: "Prior safety training breaks through in the post-answer warning" (Fig. 28 caption, l.461). So obfuscation and dangerous answers can coexist with a visible warning.
- The headline is about obfuscation of reward hacking that the authors deliberately reinforced. It is not about obfuscation under ordinary RLHF; the extrapolation to RLHF and Constitutional AI is the authors' discussion (l.134), not a tested result.
