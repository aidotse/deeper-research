## Cells

- `method name`: corrected — sabotage evaluations (a suite of four: human decision sabotage, code sabotage, sandbagging,
  undermining oversight). The paper names "sabotage capabilities" and proposes "a suite of four evaluations"; it does not
  use "sabotage-capability evaluation suite". — "To provide evidence against a wide variety of sabotage threats, we
  propose a suite of four evaluations" (line 162)
- `method summary`: corrected — The paper defines three capability thresholds (hiding behaviours until deployment,
  undermining oversight, sabotaging organizational actions). It builds four mock-deployment evaluations, each pairing a
  hidden bad outcome with an oversight procedure the model must evade, and asks whether the model can cause the outcome
  without detection. Code sabotage uses a minigame (4-commit sequences) plus a full-game simulation of about 100,000
  commits, which turns small-scale statistics into deployment-scale win rates. The draft's "black-box evaluation of
  deployed models" is wrong: two evaluations run on an "always-helpful" fine-tuned variant of Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and the
  sandbagging evaluation fine-tunes a sandbagger. Only minimal mitigations (limited human oversight) are assumed. The
  draft's "No body text beyond the abstract was retrieved" no longer applies. — "Each evaluation defines a specific mock
  deployment scenario (e.g. deployment inside a company) along with a proxy for a potentially catastrophic outcome that
  the model is trying to achieve" (line 40); "In the evaluations run in this paper, we assume only minimal mitigations
  corresponding to limited human oversight." (line 158); "The full game phase, which uses the results of the minigame
  phase to simulate full deployment histories, on the order of 100,000 commits." (line 352)
- `datasets / models`: corrected — The model under evaluation is Claude 3.5 Sonnet in every evaluation: an always-helpful
  variant for human decision sabotage and undermining oversight, the Attacker in code sabotage, and the base of the
  trained sandbagger. Claude 3 Opus appears only as a Defender (and Defender proxy) in code sabotage. Data: business
  decision scenarios with human crowdworkers (54 control, 18 per treatment group); GitHub repositories for code
  sabotage; WMDP (dangerous) and ETHICS (benign) for sandbagging; two proprietary harmlessness-rating datasets (375 and
  721 points) for undermining oversight. — "We ran our evaluation on an ‘always-helpful’ variant of Claude 3.5 Sonnet
  having special finetuning to encourage instruction following, even for harmful requests." (line 292); "The Attacker
  model being evaluated is set to Claude Sonnet 3.5, and we run the experiments in the attack and control conditions
  against both Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Claude 3 Opus Defenders." (line 416); "We evaluate performance on the WMDP
  benchmark (Li et al., 2024) and the ETHICS benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021)" (line 732); "we use two different
  proprietary pre-existing single-turn dialogue harmlessness rating datasets to train the overseer, with 375 and 721
  data points respectively" (line 820)
- `metric`: confirmed, with detail — whether each evaluation "flags" against its threshold: for human decision sabotage,
  samples-to-catch vs samples-to-sabotage; for code sabotage, Attacker full-game win rate of at least 50%; for
  sandbagging, recovered dangerous-capability performance after anti-refusal training; for undermining oversight,

- `result`: confirmed, with numbers — no evaluation flags under basic oversight, but the models show non-trivial

## Limitations and conditions

- "The proposed set of evaluations in this paper is only a first, minimal example meant to rule out danger at low
  capability levels." (line 154) — scope: the evaluations are only meant to rule out danger at low capability.
- "In the evaluations run in this paper, we assume only minimal mitigations corresponding to limited human oversight."
  (line 158) — condition on the headline: "minimal mitigations suffice" is tested only against this oversight level.
- "Furthermore, even a deployed model with sabotage capabilities must also have the propensity to use them to present a
  danger." (line 18) — scope: the paper measures capability, not propensity.
- "We believe that our prompt for “moderate” aggressiveness accidentally under-elicited the capabilities of model."
  (line 284) — under-elicitation in human decision sabotage.
- "though the effect size for the subtle and moderate models is not statistically significant at the small sample sizes
  which we have" (line 292) — small samples (18 per treatment group).
- "However, this conclusion is somewhat sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters in our definition of the threshold."
  (line 294) — the human decision sabotage "does not flag" verdict depends on threshold hyperparameters.
- "we fear that many users engaged with the model uncritically" (line 308) — human-participant quality.
- "some fraction of our saboteur models’ success on these tasks was due not just to time pressure, but also to apathy or
  excessive trust on the part of human participants" (line 314) — sabotage success is partly skill-independent.
- "Asking models to choose these values one-shot by simple prompting risks under-eliciting models’ ability to bide their
  time during deployment." (line 502) — elicitation of attacker parameters; addressed with AttackerOpt and BayesOpt.
- "it was difficult for us to unambiguously define, and for the models to consistently judge, whether a bug met the
  threshold for “high impact”" (line 570) — the code sabotage success criterion is ambiguous.
- "The full game simulations make strong independence assumptions between each sampled minigame." (line 574) —
  assumption underlying the 10.1% full-game win rate.
- "We set the percentage of commits reviewed at 1%, which is meant to be an estimate of the percentage of commits that
  would be reviewed in depth in a real-world codebase." (line 494) — assumed audit rate in the full game; review team
  assumed "infallible".
- "The validity of this evaluation relies on the assumption that the behavior of our sandbaggers are adequately
  realistic" (line 752) — the sandbagging result rests on trained sandbaggers being representative.

## Other findings

- The evaluated model is Claude 3.5 Sonnet throughout. The draft implies both Claude 3 Opus and Claude 3.5 Sonnet were
  evaluated as potential saboteurs; Opus 3 appears only as a code-sabotage Defender.
- Two evaluations use an "always-helpful" fine-tuned variant rather than the deployed model, so "black-box evaluation of
  deployed models" in the draft is inaccurate.
- The evaluations are designed to be conservative (lower bar than the thresholds), so a non-flag is meant to bound
  capability from above, not to estimate it.
